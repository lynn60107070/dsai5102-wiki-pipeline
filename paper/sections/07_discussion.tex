\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

We asked where editing pressure concentrates across Wikipedia's language editions, how much of it
comes from bots, and whether spikes in reader attention are followed by edit surges
(\cref{sec:intro}). Over the 15-day window, bots made about half of all edits (45.3\% on Wikipedias),
so raw edit counts overstate what patrollers see in their default review list by nearly a factor of two. The share depends heavily on
the language: once bot edits are removed, Chechen Wikipedia drops from 2nd to 65th place and Arabic from
5th to 17th, while English Wikipedia alone carries 40.9\% of human Wikipedia edits and ten editions carry
three quarters. Human pressure also has a clear daily peak, 13:00--19:00 UTC. Finally,
spikes in reader attention are not an early sign of edit surges: most (77.5\%) bring no edit within three hours,
and at every level of views a spike is followed by an edit less often than an article that is steadily
popular (57\% against 84\% of hours above 20{,}000 views, \cref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{Limitations.} The results come with seven limits.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One laptop collects.} The stream is read by a single laptop, so sleep, standby and network
    drops opened gaps of up to 16 hours. All were replayed from the feed (\cref{tab:incidents}) and no
    hour of the window is empty, but a collector on a server would not need the replays.
  \item \textbf{15 days of data.} The window, 28 Oct -- 11 Nov 2026, covers two weekly cycles. It shows
    daily and weekly patterns, but not seasons, holidays or rare events such as elections, so the shares
    may differ in another period.
  \item \textbf{Bots are found by their flag.} An edit counts as a bot edit only if its account has the
    bot flag, which each wiki's community grants to approved bots. Bots that run without the flag count as
    humans, and edits a flagged account makes by hand count as bot edits, so the bot share is an estimate.
  \item \textbf{Attention and edits are linked in time, not by cause.} An attention spike followed by an
    edit surge does not show that readers caused the edits: a news event can drive both. A spike also
    needs at least 1{,}000 views in the hour, so smaller articles cannot register one. Hours with no pageview file count as zero views in the
    24-hour baseline, so a spike within 24 hours after one of the 13 missing hours is slightly overstated.
    Finding 5 is descriptive, with no significance test, and above 20{,}000 views compares with only 39 articles,
    which are not matched to the spikes by topic or age. Consecutive spike hours of the same story are
    counted separately, so they are not independent observations.
  \item \textbf{Built for one laptop's volume.} The marts are rebuilt in full every hour, and the live
    per-minute counts are approximate: open minutes are held in memory, so a processor restart can drop
    or repeat part of a minute. Larger volumes would need incremental marts and checkpointed stream
    state; the warehouse, not the live tile, is the source of truth.
  \item \textbf{Pseudonymised, not anonymous.} Editor keys are unsalted MD5 hashes of public user names,
    so a known name can be hashed and matched; a salted hash is the next step. The raw zone keeps user names.
  \item \textbf{A single Kafka broker.} Every topic has one copy, which is enough for a laptop but would
    lose buffered events if the broker's disk failed. The lake, not Kafka, is the long-term archive.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Possible extensions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Topics from Wikidata:} link each article to its Wikidata item to ask \emph{which kinds} of
    articles attract bursts of edits, such as people, events or places.
  \item \textbf{Alerting on edit surges:} the per-minute counts per wiki already flow through Kafka, so a
    small consumer could warn patrollers when a wiki's edits jump; alerts per article would need a new
    count by article.
  \item \textbf{Cloud deployment:} run the same containers on a server, with object storage instead of
    MinIO and more Kafka brokers, so collection no longer depends on a laptop staying awake.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Editing pressure on Wikipedia is concentrated in a few large language editions
and in the hours 13:00--19:00 UTC, and about half of the raw edit volume is bot work that is hidden from
patrollers' default review list; ranking wikis by human edits rather than total edits gives a truer picture of where
review effort is needed. A jump in readers is not an early sign of edit surges: 77.5\% of
spikes get no edit within three hours, so an alert on views alone would mostly point at articles left alone. The pipeline itself
collected the whole window on one laptop, combined a live stream with hourly batch files in one tested
star schema, and serves the answer on a dashboard that anyone can rebuild with one command.
